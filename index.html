What I care about

It was never about
AI.
It's about who it works for.

I grew up inside systems that had already failed the people in them. So I don't get excited about new tools. I ask who they're for, who gets left out, and who pays.

New things land on a world that's already there. Where work burns people out, it burns them out faster. Where a few already hold the opportunities, they keep more of them. Where care is left to unpaid people, they carry more of it.

I studied chemistry before computer engineering, and Le Châtelier's principle still shapes how I think. Disturb a system and it pushes back to the same balance. Speed it up and it just gets there faster. The only way to a different balance is to change the conditions it lives in. That's the work: not absorbing whatever happens to us, but changing what happens next, together. See it in practice ↓

Le Châtelier in practice

Every system
pushes back.

In chemistry, a system at equilibrium pushes back against anything you do to it. Add more, take some away, squeeze it: under the same conditions it returns to the same balance, the equilibrium constant. A catalyst only gets it there faster. Only a change in the conditions, like the temperature of a reaction, moves the balance itself. Pick a disturbance and watch both happen.

In practice

    01 Disturbance

    A product team ships an AI assistant that answers customers’ questions from the company’s own documents.

    02 How the system pushes back

    • Stale docs, served fast
    • Confident wrong answers
    • Nobody owns the sources
    • Side tools nobody approved

    03 What I change

    • Clean and date the sources before they are indexed.
    • Give every source an owner who keeps it current.
    • Test it on the questions people really ask, not on demos.
    • Keep a person on every decision that matters.

    AI is a catalyst: it gets a team to the balance it already had, only faster. Change the balance first.

    01 Disturbance

    A critical vulnerability lands in a dependency, or a credential leaks.

    02 How the system pushes back

    • Panic patching
    • Blame
    • Everyone locked out
    • Alerts nobody reads

    03 What I change

    • Keep secrets out of the code, so a leak takes minutes to rotate.
    • Patch by real exposure, not by the severity score alone.
    • Write a blameless post-mortem, so people report early next time.
    • Make the safe path the easy one: SSO and least privilege.

    Locking everything down after a leak is push-back. Making the safe path the easy one is a new condition.

    01 Disturbance

    A foundation opens a grant call with a short deadline.

    02 How the system pushes back

    • Project bent to fit the money
    • Partners on paper only
    • Budget doesn’t match the work
    • Indicators nobody measures

    03 What I change

    • Start from the needs analysis, then check whether the call fits.
    • Tie each goal to a real task and a person who owns it.
    • Bring in partners with a real role and a line in the budget.
    • Ask the people it was for whether it helped.

    Bending the project to fit the call is push-back. Starting from the need changes the conditions.

    01 Disturbance

    Two people in a group clash over how something should be done, and the work stalls.

    02 How the system pushes back

    • Sides form
    • People go quiet
    • Gossip instead of feedback
    • Someone leaves

    03 What I change

    • Find out who owns the problem before anyone tries to fix it.
    • Listen actively, and say back what I heard before I answer.
    • Speak in I-messages: what I see, how it affects me, what I need.
    • Look for a solution both people accept (Gordon’s no-lose method), then agree on a check-in.

    Taking sides is push-back. A shared way to resolve conflict, that everyone knows, is a new condition.

    01 Disturbance

    A volunteer group moves its chats, files and sign-ups into one shared platform.

    02 How the system pushes back

    • WhatsApp is still the real channel
    • “I’m not technical”
    • Two places for everything
    • Only ~5 people use it

    03 What I changed

    • Document every flow, including onboarding and offboarding.
    • Keep one place for each kind of information.
    • A year later, the platform is still the one place people look.

    A new tool on old habits settles back into the old habits. Change where the work lives, not just the tool.

    Pushing harder won’t hold. Change the conditions.

    Ideas · how they grow

    A new idea arrives.

    1. 01 Disturbance

      Someone says it could be different

      Who care is for, who work serves, who gets a say. This page is one of those voices.

    2. 02 Push-back

      The system pushes back

      • “That’s just how it is”
      • “Nice, but not realistic”
      • It becomes a slogan
      • The people pushing it get tired

      Left alone, things settle back where they were.

    3. 03 What I change

      So I change the conditions

      • Make it a habit. Something people do every week, not just agree with once.
      • Build it in. Into the rules, the budget, who’s at the table.
      • Don’t carry it alone. A group survives one person leaving.
      • Pass it on. So it outlives whoever started it.

    4. 04 A new balance

      And this time it holds

      An idea on its own gets pushed back like anything else. It lasts when it changes the conditions: who decides, who gets paid, who’s in the room.

    1978 · The Basaglia law

    Italy closed its psychiatric hospitals by law. In Trieste it held, because Franco Basaglia’s team had spent years building community mental health centres to take their place. Where nobody built them, the reform stalled.

    The law was the disturbance. The centres changed the conditions.
